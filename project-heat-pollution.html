Geospatial and Multivariate Analysis of the Relationship Between Urban Air Pollutants and Heat Stress Across Indian Metropolitan

IIT Madras  • Nov'2025-June'2026
Supervisor: Dr. Chandan sarangi, IIT Madras

Developed a Python-based workflow for 550+ CPCB stations (180+ cities) with 5-year hourly data covering 11 air pollutants and five meteorological variables: ambient air temperature (AT), relative humidity (RH), wind speed (WS), wind direction (WD), and solar radiation (SR).

Workflow

Quality control worked with two outlier processing branches during development: an older MAD approach and an adaptive MAD approach. Our later discussion identified STL–MAD as the final method for the project description. The exact final threshold and all parameter settings should be taken from the final script rather than assumed.

Heat stress indicator: NOAA/NWS Heat Index calculated using near-surface air temperature (AT) and relative humidity (RH).Data: Hourly CPCB/CAAQMS station observations, merged chronologically across years without averaging, interpolation or resampling.
Pollutants analysed: PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, NO, NO₂, NOx, SO₂, CO, O₃, NH₃, benzene and toluene.

Statistical relationships performed normality assessment, Spearman correlation, scatter plots, regression and diurnal patterns to characterize associations and temporal variation.
Lag analysis also, discussed distributed lag analysis to test whether pollutant concentrations were associated with HI at the same hour or at subsequent hours.
This helps distinguish contemporaneous from delayed statistical associations; it does not, by itself, establish causation.

Results

Lag Analysis: The Distributed Lag Model (0–3 hours) revealed nonlinear relationships between NOAA Heat Index and urban air pollutants, with 2–3-hour lag effects observed for ozone (O₃) at key locations, indicating delayed associations beyond the concurrent hour.

Diurnal Analysis: Hourly profiles were examined to characterize daily variations in Heat Index and pollutant concentrations and identify periods of potentially overlapping heat stress and pollution exposure across monitoring stations.

Ahmedabad analysis Earlier plots suggested predominantly positive HI relationships with PM₂.₅, PM₁₀ and NO₂. 

Meteorological effects Wind speed appeared important in some regression models, indicating that pollutant variation cannot be explained by heat index alone.
