Explainable Machine Learning for Landslide Susceptibility, Devikulam Taluk (Idukki, Western Ghats, india)

IIT Madras • Nov'2025-June'2026Supervisor: Dr. Subbarao Pichuka, IIT Madras.Manuscript under review

Rainfall-triggered landslides are a recurring hazard in the Western Ghats of Kerala. This study maps landslide susceptibility across Devikulam Taluk (about 1,204 km², elevation up to 2,685 m). It compares four machine-learning models and then uses SHAP explainability to show why the best model makes its predictions. The aim was a map that is both accurate and understandable, so it can support hazard assessment and planning.

Workflow


1. Inventory761 mapped landslides and 761 non-landslide points, giving 1,522 samples.
2. Conditioning factorsEleven factors in four groups. Terrain: elevation, slope, aspect and terrain ruggedness index (SRTM, 30 m). Water: annual rainfall (CHIRPS v2.0) and distance to streams. Geology: lithology, geomorphology and distance to lineaments (GSI Bhukosh). Human influence: distance to roads (OpenStreetMap) and land use/land cover (GLC_FCS30D).
3. Data preparationAll layers were brought to one 30 m grid (WGS 84 / UTM Zone 43N), checked for alignment, and categorical factors were one-hot encoded.
4. Multicollinearity checkThe highest VIF was 3.12 (terrain ruggedness index), below the threshold of 5, so all factors were kept.
5. Model trainingRandom Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM and SVM were trained on a stratified 70:30 split (1,065 training and 457 test samples), with grid-search cross-validation.
6. EvaluationAccuracy, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC and precision-recall curves.
7. ExplainabilitySHAP importance and beeswarm plots, dependence plots for the six most influential factors, and LOWESS smoothing for the rainfall response.
8. MappingSusceptibility surfaces were classified into five classes using Jenks natural breaks.

Results


Model performanceXGBoost performed best, with an AUC of 0.963, accuracy of 89.3%, precision of 88.4%, recall of 90.4% and F1-score of 0.894. LightGBM was second (AUC 0.952), followed by Random Forest (AUC 0.933) and SVM (AUC 0.899).
Match with mapped landslidesAbout 94% of the mapped landslides fall in the High and Very High susceptibility classes, which together cover only about 34% of the area. The Very High class alone holds about 81% of the landslides while covering about 22% of the area. The Very Low class covers about 40% of the area and holds less than 1% of the landslides.
What drives the modelSHAP analysis identified rainfall (21.0%), distance to roads (20.0%), elevation (14.7%) and distance to streams (10.8%) as the most influential factors. Landslide susceptibility was higher at lower elevations and closer to roads, streams and lineaments. Road influence was strongest within about 1 km of the road network.
Rainfall responseRainfall does not raise susceptibility steadily. Below about 3,000 mm of annual rainfall its contribution is negative. It then rises to about 4,300 mm, stays high up to about 5,000 mm, weakens beyond about 5,200 mm and turns negative above about 5,700 mm.
Agreement between modelsAll four models gave similar spatial patterns. The High and Very High classes covered between 34.1% (XGBoost) and 37.1% (SVM) of the area.
Limits and next stepThe model uses annual rainfall and a fixed landslide inventory, so it maps long-term regional susceptibility, not individual events. The natural next step is to add event-based rainfall and soil moisture, so that susceptibility can change over time.
